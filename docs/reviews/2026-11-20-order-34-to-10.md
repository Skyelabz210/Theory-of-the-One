## From a radial limit to the reflected rational cusp

Write $e(z)=\exp(2\pi i z)$ and $(a;q)_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-aq^j)$. For a primitive root $\zeta$ of exact order $2k$, Folsom, Ono and Rhoades (FOR) prove

$$
 B_k(\zeta)=\lim_{t\uparrow1}\big[f(t\zeta)-(-1)^k b(t\zeta)\big]
 =-4\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\zeta^{n+1}(-\zeta;\zeta)_n^2,
 \qquad b(q)=\frac{(q;q)_\infty}{(-q;q)_\infty^2}.
$$

The factor $1+\zeta^k$ kills every later summand. For example, Lean evaluates the finite sums $B_1(-1)=4$ and $B_2(i)=4i$ exactly. FOR identifies them with the radial limits of $f+b$ and $f-b$, respectively. At orders 34 and 10 the half-orders 17 and 5 are both odd, so the interior functions to evaluate are $f(t\zeta)+b(t\zeta)$ in both cases. [FOR, Theorem 1.1](https://afolsom.people.amherst.edu/FolsomOnoRhoades-ForumPi.pdf#page=4) · [Exact finite evaluations](../../formal/universal/QuantumRadial.lean)

In *“Strange” combinatorial quantum modular forms*, Folsom, Ki, Truong Vu and Yang (FKTY) use two series, $F(w;q)=\sum_{n\ge0}w^{n+1}(wq;q)_n$ and $U(w;q)=\sum_{n\ge0}q^{n+1}(wq;q)_n(w^{-1}q;q)_n$. Their Corollary 1.3 gives $F(-1;q^{-1})=U(-1;q)$ at every primitive even-order root $q$. Here $-4U(-1;q)=B_k(q)$. The quantum set for their parameters $(a,b,c)=(1,2,0)$ is $S_2=\{h/d:\ d>0,\ \gcd(h,d)=1,\ 2\mid d\}$. In the normalization printed on p. 322, the resulting value at $x=h/d$ is

$$
 \phi_{1,2,0}(x)
 =-4\sqrt{12i}\,e(x/24)F(-1;e(x))
 =\sqrt{12i}\,e(x/24) B_{d/2}(e(-x)).
$$

The inverse nome fixes the sign of the cusp. A positive-angle FOR root $e(1/d)$ contributes to $\phi(-1/d)$. The principal square root fixes the constant phase. FKTY's lower-half-plane function at $z=x-iy$, $y>0$, is the Eichler integral $\vartheta^*(z)=\int_{\bar z}^{i\infty}\vartheta_{1,2,0}(u)(u-z)^{-1/2}\,du$. Its path starts at $x+iy$. They obtain the rational transformation by matching cusp asymptotics as $y\downarrow0$. The radial approach in FOR keeps $t e(-x)$ inside the unit disk. [FKTY, Corollaries 1.3–1.4 and the specialization of (1.9)](https://afolsom.people.amherst.edu/FKTY-JNT16.pdf#page=6) · [FKTY, lower-half-plane integral and proof of Theorem 1.8](https://afolsom.people.amherst.edu/FKTY-JNT16.pdf#page=26)

## The matrix, period, and differential shadow

Take $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\24&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma(24)$ and $x=-1/34$. Then $Ax=x/(24x+1)=-1/10$. Both fractions belong to $S_2$. The pole of this action is $-1/24$, and $24x+1=5/17>0$. The two characters in FKTY's multiplier have denominator 1 and $\epsilon_1=1$, so $\psi_{12}(A)=1$. Their Theorem 1.8, equation (1.13), specializes to

$$
 \sqrt{12i}\left[
 e(-1/816) B_{17}(e(1/34))
 -\sqrt{17/5}\,e(-1/240) B_5(e(1/10))
 \right]
 =\int_{-1/24}^{i\infty}\frac{\vartheta_{1,2,0}(u)}{\sqrt{u+1/34}}\,du .
$$

The period uses the vertical path $u=-1/24+it$, $t>0$. Its principal square root is evaluated at $u-x=-5/408+it$, which approaches the negative real axis from above. In the published theorem the period gives the real-analytic extension of the defect away from the pole. The matrix also appears in FKTY's Example 1, where the authors use a different input cusp, $1/2$. The earlier weight-$3/2$ result of Bryson, Ono, Pitman and Rhoades uses $U(1;q)$. Here the twist is $-1$ and the weight is $1/2$. ## Which residue data evaluate the endpoints?

$$
 \kappa=15(s-c)\bmod17=K\bmod17,\qquad
 X\bmod34=((r\bmod34)+8\kappa)\bmod34.

## Why the vertical period converges

Set $s=-1/24$, $a=\pi/12$, $g_m(t)=m\exp(-a m^2t)$ and $A_m=\varepsilon_6(m)e(-m^2/576)$, where $\varepsilon_6$ is $+1$ on $1\bmod6$, $-1$ on $5\bmod6$, and zero otherwise. The expansion at $s+it$ is $\vartheta_{1,2,0}(s+it)=2\sum_{m\ge0} A_m g_m(t)$. Shifting $m$ by 144 preserves $\varepsilon_6$ and multiplies the exponential by $(-1)^m$. Every supported $m$ is odd, so $A_{m+144}=-A_m$. Adding the shifted expansion gives the exact identity

$$
\vartheta_{1,2,0}(s+it)=\sum_{m<144}A_mg_m(t)
+\sum_{m\ge0}A_m\bigl(g_m(t)-g_{m+144}(t)\bigr).
$$

Each head term is integrable. For $m>0$ the difference has the summable bound

$$
\int_0^\infty |g_m(t)-g_{m+144}(t)|\,dt\ \le\ \frac{3\cdot144}{a m^2}.
$$

This establishes absolute integrability of the theta numerator across the entire vertical ray. For real $x\ne s$ the square-root kernel satisfies $|i/\sqrt{s+it-x}|\le |s-x|^{-1/2}$. At $x=-1/34$ the separation is $5/408$, giving the uniform bound $\sqrt{408/5}$. This proves absolute integrability of the full period at the given pair, and the same bound applies uniformly for real $x$ in a sufficiently small neighborhood disjoint from $s$. [Phase and pairing](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/FKTYCuspModes.lean) · [Integrated Gaussian bound](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/GaussianPairIntegrable.lean) · [Period integrability and regularity](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/FKTYPeriodRegularity.lean)

In fact the period is **real analytic at every $x\ne -1/24$**. On $\operatorname{Re}z<s$ the kernel extends holomorphically as $i(s+it-z)^{-1/2}$. On $\operatorname{Re}z>s$ it extends as $(z-s-it)^{-1/2}$. The two formulas recover the principal-branch integrand on their respective real intervals. A ball of radius $|\operatorname{Re}z-s|/2$ on either side gives a uniform derivative bound, so differentiation under the integral gives a holomorphic extension locally. For every reduced even-denominator rational $x>-1/24$, the theorem `fktyPhi_defect_eq_cuspPeriod` then applies the published FKTY law to identify $\phi(x)-(24x+1)^{-1/2}\phi(x/(24x+1))$ with this same real-analytic period. Absolute convergence and real analyticity on both sides of the excluded point hold independently of that transformation hypothesis. [Branch identity and real-analyticity theorem](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/FKTYPeriodRegularity.lean)

The two terminating cusp values also admit exact first-moment formulas. For $d=10,34$, set $L=24d$ and $u=e(-1/L)$. Then

$$
 \phi_{1,2,0}(-1/d)
 =2\sqrt{12i}\left(-\frac1L\sum_{m=0}^{L-1}m\,\varepsilon_6(m)u^{m^2}\right).
$$

The sum has 240 or 816 terms. The sequence $\varepsilon_6(m)u^{m^2}$ has period $L$ and sums to zero over a full period, so replacing $m/L$ by the Bernoulli polynomial $B_1(m/L)=m/L-1/2$ leaves the moment unchanged. The Lean proof groups the integral weights by exponents at the physical root and checks the resulting polynomial. It identifies the finite moments with the cusp values. ## Recovering the finite moments from the integrals

Write $c_m=\varepsilon_6(m)e(xm^2/24)$, with $x=-1/d$ and $L=24d$. Let $b>0$ be the starting height on the ray $u=x+it$. In the lower-half-plane integral the parameter is $x-ib$, so $u-(x-ib)=i(t+b)$. In the fixed-cusp integral the parameter is $x$, so $u-x=it$. For $h=\sqrt{\pi b/12}$, integration of each Gaussian mode gives

$$
 2\sqrt{12i}\sum_{m\ge0}c_m W(mh),\qquad
 W(v)=
 \begin{cases}
 e^{v^2}\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt2\,v) & \text{at the lower parameter},\\
 \operatorname{erfc}(v) & \text{at the fixed real cusp}.
 \end{cases}
$$

The positive cutoff makes the sum of the mode integrals absolutely convergent. The factor $\sqrt{12i}$ includes the phase from $du=i\,dt$ and the principal square root. [Vertical integrals and mode evaluations](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/EichlerCuspLimit.lean)

Both cutoff functions are decreasing, convex on $[0,\infty)$ and continuous at zero with $W(0)=1$. For the lower profile the second derivative simplifies to $(2+4v^2)W(v)$. To take the limit, interpolate $W(mh)$ linearly between the endpoints of each block of $L$ consecutive indices. The constant term vanishes against the zero-mean coefficients. The slope terms telescope to $-\sum_{r<L}r c_r/L$. Convexity bounds the sum of the interpolation errors in absolute value by

$$
 (1-W(h))\sum_{r<L}r|c_r|,
$$

which tends to zero. This proves that both vertical integrals converge to $\phi(-1/34)$ and $\phi(-1/10)$ at their respective cusps. ## Comparing the vertical paths

Set $x=-1/34$, $x'=-1/10$ and $s=-1/24$. The inverse image of $w=x'+it$ under $A$ is the arc $z(t)=w/(1-24w)$, running from $x$ to $s$ in the upper half-plane. The fractional-linear derivative, principal square roots and weight-$3/2$ factor for the theta series give

$$
 \frac{\vartheta_{1,2,0}(z(t))z'(t)}{\sqrt{z(t)-x}}
 =\sqrt{17/5}\,\frac{i\,\vartheta_{1,2,0}(w)}{\sqrt{it}}.
$$

The theta transformation on the upper half-plane follows by writing $\vartheta_{1,2,0}(u)$ as a shifted Jacobi theta derivative of characteristic $1/6$ at $\tau=3u$. Under $u\mapsto Au$, the inverse variable $-1/\tau$ changes by $-8$, a period of the ordinary Jacobi derivative. [Theta identity and transformation](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/FKTYThetaTransform.lean) · [Arc and change of variable](../../adapters/MockThetaAnalytic/EichlerTransportPath.lean)

The differential $\vartheta_{1,2,0}(z)\,dz/\sqrt{z-x}$ admits a holomorphic primitive on the whole upper half-plane. At either end of the arc, a point of height $y$ is horizontally displaced from the corresponding vertical ray by $O(y^2)$. The change in the primitive along a horizontal segment of length $\delta$ is at most $24\delta/(\pi y^{3/2})$. The arc and vertical ray therefore give the same endpoint value, with error $O(\sqrt y)$. At the upper ends of the vertical rays the same bound makes any fixed horizontal displacement negligible. The fundamental theorem of calculus now relates the two cusp values to the period on the ray above $s$. ## Historical scope and a next proof

Ramanujan’s January 1920 letter lists four third-order, ten fifth-order, and three seventh-order examples. He gives these series labels without a general definition of “order.” Here $f$ belongs to the third-order group at both cusps. The integers 34 and 10 are the multiplicative orders of the evaluation roots. The seventeen coefficient constructions in this repository cover the letter’s examples. For fifth-order $\chi_0$ and seventh-order $\mathcal F_0$, Lean also proves that their integer coefficient series sum to their defining hypergeometric functions throughout $|q|<1$ and that those functions are holomorphic there. The infinite differential identity $\xi_{1/2}M_f=4\Theta$ concerns the named completion with holomorphic part $q^{-1}f(q^{24})$. Griffin, Ono and Rolen report proving that Ramanujan’s examples satisfy his original criterion in their formulation. Bringmann and Rolen spell out three conditions. They require exponential growth at infinitely many roots, a modular correction $q^\alpha M_\zeta$ giving a bounded radial difference at each root $\zeta$, and the failure of one modular correction to work at every root. A Lean proof of those conditions would formalize an established theorem. Griffin, Ono and Rolen attribute the weight-$1/2$ harmonic weak Maass completions and their unary-theta period corrections to Zwegers in Fact 2.2. Their Corollary 1.2 proves that **no single** weakly holomorphic modular form of any half-integral weight on any $\Gamma_1(N')$ leaves bounded radial differences at every root of unity for a normalized Ramanujan example. This is compatible with FOR’s result here. FOR allows the sign of the compensating term to depend on the cusp, and evaluates the resulting limit. They expand $1/\Phi_{10}$ in a Fourier–Jacobi series and split a meromorphic coefficient as $\psi_m=\psi_m^F+\psi_m^P$. The **finite part** $\psi_m^F$ is holomorphic in the elliptic variable and still has an infinite Fourier expansion. For the single-centered quarter-BPS charges, with $4mn-\ell^2>0$, the indexed degeneracy is extracted from $[q^n y^\ell]\psi_m^F$ with physical sign $(-1)^{\ell+1}$. The same series also has negative-discriminant terms. DMZ explicitly exclude those terms from the single-centered interpretation. The polar part accounts for wall crossing. Entropy concerns the logarithmic growth of the appropriate degeneracies.
